Paris, thirteen oh three. You are twelve years old, and this morning your father is giving you away.

Not forever. For eight years.

He walks you through streets you have never been down, to a narrow house with a wide front room. In that room stands a wooden frame as long as a door, and across it someone has stretched a piece of silk. A man is bent over it, pushing a needle through with thread that catches the light. Gold.

That man is your new master. From today, you live in his house, you eat at his table, and you learn his trade.

So let us put you in it.

The first thing to understand is that nobody in Paris simply hires a boy. There is a contract, and there are rules about the contract.

A generation ago, in the time of King Louis, the provost of Paris, Étienne Boileau, had the rules of the city's trades written down. Over a hundred trades. Each one has its own rules, and each one has sworn men called jurés, whose job is to make sure those rules are kept.

Before your contract is made, the jurés ask questions. Not about you. About him. Can he actually do the work? Does he have the money to feed and train an apprentice for years? A master who is bad at his craft, or too poor, is not allowed to take you.

And he can only take one of you. A master may train his own children without limit. But a boy from another family usually gets one place in a workshop. One. You are that place.

The contract is made in front of witnesses: other masters, and men who work in the trade. Everyone hears the terms.

Here is the part nobody tells you. Your family pays for this. The apprentice does not earn. The apprentice costs. In some trades, the shorter the term your family asks for, the more they have to pay. Sign for a long term, and it can cost almost nothing, because your master gets years of your work back.

Your trade is embroidery. And the embroiderers of Paris have just been given new statutes, this very year. They set the minimum for an apprentice. Eight years.

So. Eight years.

Your first day starts when it gets light. Not at an hour. There is no clock in this house. There is a window.

In a trade like this, the working day is the daylight. Work starts when there is enough light to see a thread, and it stops when there is not. In June, that is a long day. In December, it is a short one, and nothing anyone does will make it longer.

Because here is the rule that tells you everything about this job. The embroiderers of Paris are forbidden to work by candlelight.

Not because your master is kind. Because the provost has decided that work done by a candle is worse than work done by day, and Paris embroidery is not allowed to be worse.

Night work is banned in many trades in this city. A candle hides mistakes. Daylight shows them. And the rules care very much about what other people will see.

So you will not touch the silk today. You will not touch it for a long time. You fetch things. You wind thread. And mostly, you watch. Watching is the work of the first year.

Your week has a shape. Six days at the frame, and then Sunday, when nobody works. Feast days, nobody works. The church calendar is the calendar of work.

And Saturday ends early. In most trades in Paris, work stops on Saturday at the first bell of vespers, in the late afternoon. When that bell rings across the roofs, the needle goes down, wherever it is in the stitch.

Then, one morning, there is a knock at the door.

Jurés. Men from the trade, sworn to inspect. They have the right to walk into any workshop of their craft and look at the work. The thread. The materials. The finish.

If something is wrong, they can seize it. The master pays a fine. A bad piece is not a private mistake. It is evidence.

This is not a raid. This is the trade doing its job. Every piece that leaves this house carries the name of every embroiderer in Paris. If a buyer is cheated here, he will not trust the next workshop either.

Your master stands very still while they look. You learn something about him that day.

The years pass the way years do in a workshop. Slowly, then all at once. One day you are allowed to hold the needle. Then to work a plain part of the background. Then a part someone will actually look at.

And at the end of eight years, you are twenty. You are not a master. You are a journeyman.

A journeyman is paid by the day. He can work for any master who will hire him. For the first time in your life, you can leave a house because you want to.

To become a master yourself, you need three things. The skill, which you now have. The money to set up your own workshop, which you almost certainly do not. And the oath, sworn before the jurés, that you will keep the rules of the trade. In some trades, you also have to buy the right to work at all.

So most journeymen stay journeymen for a long time. Some of them, forever.

But think back to the twelve-year-old who walked into this room. You could not thread a needle with gold. You did not know what a juré was.

Now you know what Paris embroidery is worth, and why.

And every evening, for eight years, when the window went grey, you put the needle down. Nobody lit a candle. Not because candles were expensive. Because that was the law.

That is what an ordinary life is. Not small. Just made, very carefully, in daylight.

And if you want more of these, one ordinary life at a time, subscribing is the whole business model of this channel.
