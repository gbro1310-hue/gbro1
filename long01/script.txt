In Rome, the higher you live, the less you pay. Not because the view is worse. Because when the building burns, you are the last one out.

So let us put you in it. Rome, around the year one hundred. A million people. And you have just rented a room on the fifth floor.

The building is called an insula. An island. A block of rooms stacked on top of each other, wrapped around a narrow courtyard. A list of the city from a few centuries later counts about forty-six thousand of these blocks. Private houses? Fewer than two thousand. This is not the poor part of Rome. This is Rome.

At street level, shops. A baker, a wine counter, a man who fixes sandals. Above them, the good apartments. Big rooms, high ceilings, maybe glass in the windows.

Then every floor gets cheaper. Lower ceilings. Smaller rooms. Thinner walls. By the time the stairs reach you, the rooms are wooden boxes under the roof. Your window has no glass. It has a wooden shutter. Open, you get light and noise. Closed, you get dark.

Augustus tried to limit these buildings to about twenty metres. That rule exists because higher buildings kept falling down.

Here's the part nobody tells you. The landlord knows. When a crack appears in the wall, he does not rebuild. He paints over it. A Roman poet joked that the whole city was standing on thin props, and that the manager would plaster a crack and tell you to sleep well while the building hung over your head.

Morning. You are thirsty. There is no water in your room. There is no water on your floor.

Rome has some of the best water in the ancient world. Aqueducts bring it from the hills into public fountains on the street. Clean, cold, free. Five floors down.

So you take a bucket and go down. You fill it. Ten litres is ten kilos. You carry it back up five flights of narrow stairs. That is your water for drinking, washing your face, and cleaning the cup. If you want more, you go again. This is not hardship. This is Tuesday.

Now the other problem. There is no toilet up here either.

You have a pot. When it is full, you have two choices. You carry it down to the big jar under the stairwell, where the building collects it. That jar does not go to waste. A fuller will buy it, because his laundry runs on urine.

Or you open the shutter. People did. Often enough that Roman law had rules about who pays when something thrown from a window hits a person in the street. The same poet warned that walking out to dinner without making a will was careless, because every open window above you at night was a risk.

Breakfast. You have no kitchen. Cooking up here means a small charcoal burner on a wooden floor, in a wooden room, in a building full of people. Some do it. Most do not.

You go down again, to the street counter. A stone bar with round jars set into it. Hot lentils, bread, cheap wine mixed with water. You eat standing up. This is not a takeaway habit. For most of the city, the street is the kitchen.

Then you work. In a shop downstairs, or at the river docks. Your room is not a place to spend a day. It is a place to keep your things and sleep.

In the afternoon, you wash at the public baths. Entry costs a quadrans, the smallest coin in Rome. For a few hours, a man from the fifth floor gets the same hot water as everyone else.

Then the light goes orange, and the street changes.

Julius Caesar banned most wagons from the streets of Rome during the day. Too crowded. So every delivery happens at night. Building stone. Grain. Barrels. Iron wheels on stone streets, under your window, until dawn. The poet again: in Rome, he said, only the rich can afford to sleep.

You light a small clay oil lamp. It is a flame, in a wooden room, five floors up.

And this is the thing everyone in the building thinks about and no one says. Fire.

Fires break out all the time. Augustus created a fire brigade, the vigiles. Several thousand men, patrolling at night with buckets, ropes, axes and blankets soaked in vinegar. That is the whole system. There is no pump that reaches the fifth floor. There is a line of people passing buckets.

So when you smell smoke, you do not wait. The stairs are the only way out. The people below you are already on them.

A century and a half earlier, a rich man named Crassus understood this better than anyone. When a block caught fire, he would arrive with his own workers and offer to buy the building on the spot, cheaply, while it burned. If the owner agreed, his men put the fire out.

Then there is the rent. It is due once a year, and the first of July was the traditional day. Miss it, and you are out, with whatever you can carry down five flights of stairs.

So this is your life. Water you carry. A pot you carry. Food you buy at a counter. A bath you share with half the city. A room you sleep in and leave. And every night, a lamp you blow out carefully, because nobody is coming up to save you.

That is what an ordinary life is. Not small. Not sad. Just built entirely out of things that had to be done today.
