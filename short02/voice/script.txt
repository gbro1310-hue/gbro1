In Pompeii, bread is not something you make at home. You buy it. The town has more than thirty bakeries.

So let us put you in one. You start before dawn. Grain goes into a stone mill shaped like an hourglass. A donkey walks it in circles. For hours.

The flour is never quite clean. Tiny bits of stone end up in every loaf. People who ate this bread for a lifetime wore their teeth down.

You shape round loaves and cut them into eight pieces, so they break by hand. Then you stamp them. Your name, or your master's. In nearby Herculaneum, one loaf still reads: Celer, slave of Quintus Granius Verus.

In 79 AD, one baker in Pompeii put eighty-one loaves in the oven. Then Vesuvius erupted.

They were found in 1862. Still in the oven. Burnt to charcoal. Still cut into eight.

That is what an ordinary life leaves behind. Not a statue. A morning's work.
